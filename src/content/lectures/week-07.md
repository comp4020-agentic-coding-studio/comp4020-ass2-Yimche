The status bar is the most honest object on a riced desktop, because it is where
the gap between usefulness and decoration is smallest and most visible. It
promises information. This lecture asks how much of that information anyone
actually consumes. The CPU temperature updating every second, the memory graph,
the current track, the weather in a city you are already standing in: each was
added because it could be, and each is read approximately never.

That is not a reason to sneer. It is a design problem worth studying on its own
terms. A good bar is an exercise in information density: what earns a permanent
place in your field of view, what belongs in a menu you open on demand, and what
is there purely because the empty space felt wrong. We look at the tools, at the
temptation to build a dashboard for a life that does not generate that much data,
and at the rare, disciplined bar that shows only what its owner will look at.

## In this lecture

- information density and the economics of permanent screen space
- the modules everyone adds and no one reads
- glanceable versus on-demand: what a bar should surface
- restraint as the hardest thing to put in a status bar

## Before next week

Build a bar, then remove every module you did not glance at for a whole day. Keep
a note of what you removed and how it felt.
